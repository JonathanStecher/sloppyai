# Quality benchmarks and a product's own direction

Use this when a user asks for better taste, a stronger result, work “as good as”
a reference, or a repair after an earlier redesign still feels generic.

## Establish what the reference means

“As good as” sets an execution-quality target. Inspect what makes the reference
work: offer clarity, reading order, meaningful visual evidence, interaction,
typographic control, detail and continuity across states. Translate observed
strengths into criteria for the user's product. A reference's black canvas,
serif, palette, hero layout or prompt box is not itself a quality criterion.

Use its style when the user actually requests that direction. Preserve accepted
brand choices and intentional conventional interfaces. A shared component library
or an explicit brand-system match can be appropriate; novelty is not the goal.
If the reference is unavailable, mark that comparison unassessed and continue
improvements supported by the artifact and brief. Do not invent observations.

The optional `designBrief.referenceUse` is `quality` by default, including for
older briefs with `references`. `style` records an explicit style direction.
Configuration is preference data, not new permission or evidence of user approval.

## Choose a direction that belongs to this product

Connect audience, primary task and real content to concrete decisions about type,
colour roles, imagery, density and composition. Explain why those decisions help
this product. “Modern”, “premium”, “clean” or “less AI” alone do not give a direction.
Avoid substituting a default minimal interface or a fashionable editorial serif.

When the direction is unresolved and the change is substantial, compare materially
different approaches using the same content before committing. A short description,
sketch or small prototype may suffice. Retain an already accepted direction instead
of repeating this exercise for ordinary repairs.

Let actual work, product evidence and the visitor's decision determine the sequence
of the page. Repeated feature cards, a giant slogan or an input-led hero earn their
place through that task. Removing familiar patterns or swapping a font and palette
does not by itself establish a stronger design.

## Critique the actual result

Inspect the whole page and meaningful details at matched desktop and narrow widths.
Use D24–D31 to locate concrete weaknesses in palette, optical type, imagery, spatial
rhythm, components, states and character. Recheck meaningful interactions as well.

Ask whether the result communicates this product's offer and work, whether the eye
finds the intended next action, and whether supporting content adds substance.
As a heuristic, consider how much could be reused for an unrelated product just by
swapping its name. A conventional professional tool may legitimately share a
structure; explain the fit instead of forcing decorative originality.

If the user rejects the result, identify the failed design assumption and address
it. A first generated direction is not automatically an accepted identity. Use
[research-informed design decisions](design-decisions.md) to review the named
bundle of type, colours, overlines, buttons and icons, keeping functional meaning.
Do not repeat the same structure in new colours, import the benchmark's identity,
or present a clean source scan as a rebuttal to visual feedback.

Finish the scoped visual review only with current rendered evidence, a rationale
against the product brief and reference criteria, and preserved useful behavior.
Mark each step complete, partial or not assessed. Keep unavailable evidence and
remaining weaknesses visible. Attribute taste judgments; never manufacture an
automatic originality score, a claim of parity with the benchmark, or visual approval
from zero static findings. Observe the existing bounded retry and regression rules.
